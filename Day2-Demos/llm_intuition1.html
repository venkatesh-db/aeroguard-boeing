Tokens: how an LLM "reads"

An LLM doesn't see letters or whole words. It chops text into tokens (word pieces) and turns each into a number ID.
0characters
0words
0tokens
💡 Rough rule: 1 token ≈ ¾ of an English word. Common words are one token; rare or long words are split into pieces. Hindi and other scripts often need more tokens. That matters because LLMs charge per token and have a token limit (the "context window").
Simplified tokenizer for teaching. Real tokenizers (BPE) learn their pieces from data, so exact splits differ by model.

Embeddings: meaning as a location

Each token becomes a list of numbers: a point on a "meaning map". Words with similar meaning land close together. Click any word.

💡 Real models use hundreds to thousands of numbers per token, not 2. We squash them onto a flat map so humans can see them. This is also how "semantic search" finds documents that mean the same thing even when the words differ.

Attention: which words matter to each other?

To understand a word, the model looks back at the other words and decides how much to "pay attention" to each. Click a word to see where it looks.
Click the word "it".
💡 Same word "it", different sentence, different meaning. Attention lets the model work that out from context. A Transformer (the "T" in GPT) runs many attention "heads" in parallel, stacked in dozens of layers.

The next-word engine

At its core, GPT does one thing again and again: predict the next token, add it, repeat. Click a word to choose it, or let the model pick.
💡 Temperature controls creativity. At 0 the model always picks the top word, so it's predictable. Higher temperatures give more surprising, varied text. This is also why an LLM can confidently write something plausible but wrong: it picks likely words, not checked facts.

BERT → GPT: two ways to use a Transformer

Both came from the 2017 Transformer. BERT reads the whole sentence to understand it; GPT reads left to right to generate.
📖 BERT (Google, 2018): the reader
Trained to fill in the blank, looking at words on both sides.
Good at: search, classifying text, spotting names in documents (understanding)
✍️ GPT (OpenAI, 2018 →): the writer
Trained to predict the next word, looking only at words before it.
Good at: writing, chatting, summarising, coding (generating)
